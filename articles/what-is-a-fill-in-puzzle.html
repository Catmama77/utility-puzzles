What Is a Fill-In Puzzle? A Complete Beginner's Guide

By UtilityPuzzles · August 2026

If you've ever picked up a puzzle book and found a grid full of little squares with a list of words beside it — but no clues — you've found a fill-in puzzle. Fill-ins (also called word fit, fill-it-in or word fill puzzles) are one of the most popular pencil-and-paper puzzles in newspapers and puzzle books, and they're wonderfully simple to learn.

How a fill-in puzzle works

A fill-in puzzle has three parts:

  • The grid — a square or rectangle of cells, some filled in solid black. The white cells form connected runs of different lengths: these are the slots.
  • The word list — every word you need is given to you, usually grouped by length and numbered to match the slots.
  • Crossing letters — where a horizontal slot and a vertical slot cross, they share a cell, so a letter you place correctly helps you solve the other word too.

Your job is to figure out which word from the list goes into which slot. Because the words are listed by length, counting the cells in a slot is usually your first move.

Word fill-in vs. number fill-in

There are two main flavours. In a word fill-in, the slots hold words from a category like animals, food or countries. In a number fill-in (sometimes called a number fit or figure fit puzzle), the slots hold numbers instead — usually grouped by how many digits they have. The logic is identical; only the content changes.

Why fill-ins are so popular

Unlike a crossword, a fill-in doesn't test general knowledge — no trivia required. Everything you need is printed right there in the word list. That makes fill-ins a fantastic choice for:

  • Beginners — there are no clues to decode, just fitting and logic.
  • Kids and language learners — the word list reinforces spelling and vocabulary.
  • Relaxation — many solvers find the rhythm of counting slots and matching lengths deeply calming.

The one rule to remember

Every slot has exactly one word from the list, and every word in the list is used exactly once. When you've placed everything, the grid is completely filled and the list is empty. That simple contract makes the puzzle always solvable — and always fair.
